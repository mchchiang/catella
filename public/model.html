Nucleosome Occupancy Model

Introduction

In this document, we explore how to use methylation footprinting data to infer nucleosome positioning via statistical modeling. The input of the model is the methylation footprinting dataset, such as that from fiber-seq, where we have a table of \(N_{\text{mol}}\) molecules with length \(L\) base pair (bp), storing the confidence score in calling individual bases methylated. The output of the model is a set of likely nucleosome positions for each molecule based on the methylation data. We use Bayesian inference and biophysical modeling to sample the nucleosome positions by running Monte Carlo simulations.

Model

We consider a DNA fiber of length \(L\), with position along the fiber described by the discrete coordinate \(x \in \{0,1,\dots,L-1\}\). Along the fiber, we place \(N\) nucleosomes, each leaving a footprint of \(\ell_{\text{nuc}} = 147\) bp, and their left-aligned positions form the configuration set

(1)\[\mathcal{C} = \{x_1 < x_2 < \dots < x_N\} \;,\]

where \(|x_{i+1}-x_i| \geq \ell_{\text{nuc}}\).

Our idea is to start with an initial configuration \(\mathcal{C}_0\) (e.g., a set of randomly positioned nucleosomes) and use Bayesian inference to “improve” the configuration given the methylation footprinting data. In other words, we want to sample the posterior probability \(P(\mathcal{C}|\text{data})\) based on the likelihood of observing the data given the current configuration \(P(\text{data}|\mathcal{C})\) and the prior probability of the configuration \(P(\mathcal{C})\). By Bayes’ theorem, we have

(2)\[P(\mathcal{C}|\text{data}) = \frac{P(\text{data}|\mathcal{C})\,P(\mathcal{C})}{P(\text{data})} \;.\]

Here, \(P(\text{data})\) is the evidence of observing the methylation footprinting data. To make the connection with statistical mechanics, we take the logarithm and exponentiate the right-hand side (RHS) of the equation to rewrite the posterior as a Boltzmann distribution:

(3)\[P(\mathcal{C}|\text{data}) = \frac{1}{Z}\exp[-\beta H(\mathcal{C},\vec{\theta})] \;,\]

where \(\beta = 1/(k_BT)\) is the inverse temperature, and \(\vec{\theta} = (\theta_1,\theta_2,\dots)\) is a vector of any additional model parameters we use to link between the configuration and methylation data. Moreover, one can identify the normalization factor (i.e., partition function) \(Z\) being proportional to the evidence \(P(\text{data})\) and the effective Hamiltonian taking the form

(4)\[\beta H(\mathcal{C},\vec{\theta}) = -\left[\log P(\text{data}|\mathcal{C},\vec{\theta}) + \log P(\mathcal{C}) + \text{const.}\right] \;.\]

This link between Bayes’ theorem and statistical mechanics provides the foundation of our sampling framework. We seek to find an ensemble of nucleosome configurations that satisfies the posterior, and we have just demonstrated that this is equivalent to sampling a Boltzmann distribution with an effective Hamiltonian, which can be done via Monte Carlo simulations. We will now discuss in turn how we model the prior \(P(\mathcal{C})\) and the likelihood \(P(\text{data}|\mathcal{C})\), before explaining in more detail the sampling algorithm.

Prior probability – the biophysical model of a nucleosome array

The prior probability describes the chance of observing a particular configuration assuming we have no knowledge of the experimental data. We build this probability based on the biophysics and thermodynamics of how nucleosomes interact with the DNA fiber. Since nucleosomes can bind and be removed from the fiber, the natural statistical ensemble here is the grand canonical ensemble, and so the prior is given by

(5)\[P(\mathcal{C}) = \frac{1}{Z_0}\,\exp[-\beta H_0(\mathcal{C})] \;,\]

where \(Z_0 = \sum_{\mathcal{C}}\exp[-\beta H_0(\mathcal{C})]\), and \(H_0\) is the baseline Hamiltonian of the system and has two contributions:

(6)\[H_0(\mathcal{C}) = E_{\text{rep}}(\mathcal{C}) - \mu N \;.\]

Here, the first term \(E_{\text{rep}}(\mathcal{C})\) represents the steric repulsion between nucleosomes (i.e., their footprints on the DNA cannot overlap), and the second term takes into account the changes in the number of nucleosomes along the fiber, tuned by the chemical potential \(\mu\) (note that a positive \(\mu\) means it is favourable for nucleosomes to bind to the fiber). For the pairwise repulsion between nucleosomes (say \(i\) and \(j\)), we consider a hard core potential with a Weeks-Chandler-Andersen (WCA) drop-off to model the linker length:

(7)\[\begin{split}E_{\text{rep}}(x_{ij}) = \begin{cases} \infty & \text{if } x_{ij} < \ell_{\text{nuc}} \\ \epsilon_{\text{link}}\left[\left(\frac{\ell_{\text{link}}} {x_{ij}-\ell_{\text{nuc}}}\right)^6 - 1\right]^2 & \text{if } \ell_{\text{nuc}} \leq x_{ij} \leq \ell_{\text{nuc}}+\ell_{\text{link}} \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \;, \end{cases}\end{split}\]

where \(x_{ij} = |x_i-x_j|\) is the separation between the nucleosomes, \(\ell_{\text{nuc}} = 147\) bp, \(\ell_{\text{link}}\) is the typical length of the linker DNA between nucleosomes, and \(\epsilon_{\text{link}}\) is an energy scale that sets the strength of the repulsion. With this repulsion potential, only nearest-neighbour interactions are effective and so

(8)\[E_{\text{rep}} = \sum_{i=0}^{N-2}E_{\text{rep}}^{i,i+1}\]

for \(N \geq 2\) and 0 otherwise.

Emission model data likelihood

To determine the likelihood of observing the data for a particular configuration, we need to build a model relating methylation calling and nucleosome occupancy, while accounting for the fact that there could be false positives in the calling. We note that not all sites are assayable (i.e., some bases cannot be methylated), and those that are may still not emit any signal by the base caller. We denote \(\mathcal{A}\) as the set of sites that are assayable and have emitted a call, and the model only considers these sites. For each \(x \in \mathcal{A}\) along the fiber, there are three key observables:

(9)\[\sigma_x \rightarrow m_x \rightarrow s_x \;.\]

Here, \(\sigma_x\) is the nucleosome occupancy state, indicating whether a nucleosome is covering the site (\(\sigma_x = 1\)) or not (\(\sigma_x = 0\)). Next, \(m_x\) is the methylation state, representing whether the DNA methyltransferase (MTase) has marked the site (\(m_x = 1\)) or not (\(m_x = 0\)), which depends on \(\sigma_x\). Finally, \(s_x\) is the actual signal (e.g., ionic current from nanopore sequencing) output by the base caller based on the methylation state \(m_x\) of the site. Both \(\sigma_x\) and \(m_x\) are hidden to us, and we only observe their impact via \(s_x\).

With these observables, we can define the conditional \(p(s_x|\sigma_x)\) – the probability of seeing the data signal \(s_x\) given the nucleosome state \(\sigma_x\). The overall data likelihood is then the product of this conditional across all \(x \in \mathcal{A}\), i.e.,

(10)\[P(\text{data}|\mathcal{C},\vec{\theta}) = \prod_{x\in\mathcal{A}} p(s_x|\sigma_x(\mathcal{C})) \;.\]

In doing so, we have made the assumption that the methylation calling of individual sites are independent. This is a simplification that we revisit below, as MTase processivity correlates nearby events. We introduce two model parameters \(\vec{\theta} = (\theta_P,\theta_A)\) to help us calculate the conditional \(p(s_x|\sigma_x)\). We let \(\theta_P\) be the probability that a site \(x \in \mathcal{A}\) is called methylated when protected (i.e., occupied by a nucleosome), and \(\theta_A\) the same when the site is accessible, with \(\theta_A \gg \theta_P\). It should be noted that \(\theta_P\) is not exactly the false positive rate (FPR) as nucleosomes can “breathe” and be partially unwrapped, and so \(\theta_P\) is typically higher than the FPR. Letting

(11)\[\theta_{\sigma_x} = \sigma_x\theta_P + (1-\sigma_x)\theta_A \;,\]

we can now write down the conditional \(p(s_x|\sigma_x)\) as follows:

(12)\[\begin{split}p(s_x|\sigma_x) &= \sum_{m_x\in\{0,1\}}p(s_x|m_x)\,p(m_x|\sigma_x) \\ &= p(s_x|m_x = 1)\,\theta_{\sigma_x} + p(s_x|m_x=0)\,(1-\theta_{\sigma_x}) \;.\end{split}\]

Hence, the overall data likelihood can be written as

(13)\[P(\text{data}|\mathcal{C},\vec{\theta}) = \prod_{x\in\mathcal{A}} \left[p(s_x|m_x = 1)\,\theta_{\sigma_x} + p(s_x|m_x=0)\,(1-\theta_{\sigma_x})\right] \;.\]

When sampling the posterior, it is more useful to compare the data likelihood of a given configuration with that for a reference state, which we take to be the null configuration \(\emptyset\) where there is no nucleosome occupying the fiber. In this null configuration, the likelihood \(P(\text{data}|\emptyset,\vec{\theta})\) is the same as above but with \(\theta_{\sigma_x} = \theta_A\), as all sites are accessible. Therefore, the ratio of the likelihoods becomes

(14)\[\begin{split}\frac{P(\text{data}|\mathcal{C},\vec{\theta})} {P(\text{data}|\emptyset,\vec{\theta})} = \prod_{\substack{x\in\mathcal{A} \\ \sigma_x =1}} \frac{p(s_x|m_x = 1)\,\theta_P + p(s_x|m_x=0)\,(1-\theta_P)} {p(s_x|m_x = 1)\,\theta_A + p(s_x|m_x=0)\,(1-\theta_A)} \;,\end{split}\]

where we have used the fact that the numerator cancels with the denominator for sites that are accessible (\(\sigma_x = 0\)). Defining

(15)\[L_x = \frac{p(s_x|m_x=1)}{p(s_x|m_x=0)} \;,\]

we can then rewrite the ratio as

(16)\[\begin{split}\frac{P(\text{data}|\mathcal{C},\vec{\theta})} {P(\text{data}|\emptyset,\vec{\theta})} = \prod_{\substack{x\in\mathcal{A} \\ \sigma_x =1}} \frac{L_x\theta_P + 1-\theta_P}{L_x\theta_A + 1-\theta_A} \equiv \prod_{\substack{x\in\mathcal{A} \\ \sigma_x =1}} \exp(\lambda_x(\vec{\theta})) \;.\end{split}\]

While both \(p(s_x|m_x=1)\) and \(p(s_x|m_x=0)\) in \(L_x\) are quantities we cannot directly measure, we do however know \(q_x = p(m_x=1|s_x)\), which is the confidence score (ranging between 0 and 1) the base caller outputs when calling a site methylated given the raw signal it has seen (this is the mod_qual field when extracting methylation data from a BAM file using ModKit). Assuming a prior probability \(\pi_0 = p(m_x=1)\) that the site \(x\) is methylated, we relate \(q_x\) to the unknowns above using Bayes’ theorem:

(17)\[q_x = p(m_x=1|s_x) = \frac{\pi_0\,p(s_x|m_x=1)} {\pi_0\,p(s_x|m_x=1) + (1-\pi_0)\,p(s_x|m_x=0)} \;.\]

Considering the expression for \(1-q_x\) and after some rearrangement, we find

(18)\[L_x(q_x,\pi_0) = \frac{p(s_x|m_x=1)}{p(s_x|m_x=0)} = \frac{q_x}{1-q_x}\cdot\frac{1-\pi_0}{\pi_0}\;.\]

We note that the prior \(\pi_0\) is based on the base caller’s training and is not directly reported. Lacking this information, we adopt the default uninformative choice \(\pi_0 = 1/2\), giving \(L_x = q_x/(1-q_x)\) (this can however be adjusted in the model via the pi0 argument). Finally, putting everything together, we find the logarithm of the likelihood ratio to be

(19)\[\begin{split}\log\left(\frac{P(\text{data}|\mathcal{C},\vec{\theta})} {P(\text{data}|\emptyset,\vec{\theta})}\right) \equiv \Lambda(\mathcal{C},\vec{\theta}) = \sum_{\substack{x\in\mathcal{A} \\ \sigma_x =1}} \lambda_x(\vec{\theta}) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} \lambda_i(\vec{\theta})\;,\end{split}\]

where we have regrouped the sum to be done by nucleosome (rather than by sites) and defined

(20)\[\begin{split}\lambda_i(\vec{\theta}) = \sum_{\substack{x\in\mathcal{A} \\ x \in [x_i,x_i+\ell_{\text{nuc}})}} \log\left(\frac{L_x(q_x,\pi_0)\,\theta_P + 1-\theta_P} {L_x(q_x,\pi_0)\,\theta_A + 1-\theta_A}\right) \;.\end{split}\]

This is helpful as it shows that the log-likelihood ratio enters into the Hamiltonian as a one-body interaction that can be pre-computed before running the simulations, and the full Hamiltonian now reads as

(21)\[\beta H(\mathcal{C},\vec{\theta}) = -\Lambda(\mathcal{C},\vec{\theta}) + \beta \left[E_{\text{rep}}(\mathcal{C}) - \mu N\right] + \text{const.} \;,\]

where the constant at the end now includes the term \(\log P(\text{data}|\emptyset,\vec{\theta})\) that is irrelevant for sampling. What remains to be done is estimating the parameters \(\vec{\theta} = (\theta_P,\theta_A)\) that we have introduced here. This can be carried out via two approaches: (i) performing control experiments on bare (non-chromatinized) DNA fibers and (ii) estimating these parameters by maximizing the data likelihood. The main advantage of (i) is that it allows us to account for sequence biases and introduce spatial variation in the parameters \(\vec{\theta} = \vec{\theta}(x)\), increasing the sensitivity of the model to the data. However, control experiments are not practical for cases such as probing nucleosome positioning genome-wide, where one would have to resort to approach (ii). We now discuss these two approaches in turn.

Estimating model parameters with control experiments

We perform two control experiments to estimate \(\vec{\theta}\):

  1. An experiment with a saturated level of methyltransferase (MTase) so that all assayable sites are methylated.

  2. An experiment without MTase so that all assayable sites remain unmethylated.

The first experiment allows us to estimate \(\theta_A\) – the probability of an assayable site being accessible and called methylated. This is because we have a bare DNA fiber where all bases are accessible, and the assayable ones are all methylated due to the saturated level of MTase. Let \(N_x\) be the number of molecules at an assayable site \(x\) that have emitted a signal and \(q_{x,\alpha}^{(Y)}\) be the confidence score of the methylation calling at site \(x\) for molecule \(\alpha\) in experiment \(Y \in \{a,b\}\). We write the estimator of \(\theta_A\) as

(22)\[\begin{split}\hat{\theta}_A(x) &= \frac{\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{\text{mol}}} q_{x,\alpha}^{(a)} + \nu \left\langle\hat{\theta}_{A}(x) \right\rangle}{N_x + \nu} \;, \\ &\quad\text{with}\quad \left\langle\hat{\theta}_A(x)\right\rangle = \frac{1}{N_{\text{site}}}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{A}} \sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{\text{mol}}}\frac{q_{x,\alpha}^{(a)}}{N_x} \;,\end{split}\]

where \(N_{\text{site}}\) is the total number of assayable sites along the fiber. We have introduced a regularization component in this fraction so that when the number of molecules is low, \(\theta_A(x)\) tends towards its mean across all \(x\in\mathcal{A}\), i.e., \(\left\langle\theta_A(x)\right\rangle\). This tendency is controlled by \(\nu\), the number of molecules present before variation in \(\theta_A\) across \(x\) becomes favoured.

The second experiment tells us about the false positive rate (FPR). Since there is no MTase present, none of the sites should have been called methylated; yet, the base caller may still emit a call for some sites by chance, producing false positives. The FPR can be estimated in the same manner as for \(\theta_A\), leading to

(23)\[\begin{split}\text{FPR}(x) &= \frac{\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{\text{mol}}} q_{x,\alpha}^{(b)} + \nu \left\langle\text{FPR}(x)\right\rangle} {N_x + \nu} \;, \\ &\quad\text{with}\quad \left\langle\text{FPR}(x)\right\rangle = \frac{1}{N_{\text{site}}}\sum_{x\in\mathcal{A}} \sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{\text{mol}}}\frac{q_{x,\alpha}^{(b)}}{N_x}\;.\end{split}\]

The FPR is closely related to \(\theta_P\), the probability that an assayable site protected by a nucleosome is called methylated. This is because the setup of this experiment can be thought of as an approximation of the condition where all sites are covered by nucleosomes and thus cannot be methylated. Nevertheless, the two conditions are not exactly equivalent as nucleosomes can breathe, and the partial unwrapping allows some “leakage” for methylation. As such, we expect \(\theta_P > \text{FPR}\) and estimate the former as

(24)\[\hat{\theta}_P(x) = \text{FPR}(x) + \rho\, [\hat{\theta}_A(x) - \text{FPR}(x)] \;,\]

where \(\rho\) describes the level of leakage of methylation signal. The default values used in the simulation framework for \(\nu\) and \(\rho\) are 10 and 0.1, respectively.

Estimating model parameters by maximizing data likelihood

As mentioned above, it is often not feasible to perform control experiments. In this scenario, we try to determine a set of parameters that maximize the data likelihood instead. To make progress, we divide the DNA into non-overlapping bins, where each bin has the size of a nucleosome \(\ell_{\text{nuc}}\). We choose non-overlapping bins instead of rolling-window bins because the latter inflates the number of observations and makes the estimated parameters look more certain than they actually are. We pool together all molecules so that the total number of bins \(N_{\text{bin}}\) is the product of the number of molecules \(N_{\text{mol}}\) and the maximum number of bins per molecule. Each bin \(w = 1,2,\dots,N_{\text{bin}}\) is described by three attributes: (i) the number of assayable sites with an emitted signal \(M_w\), (ii) the “number” of which are likely to be methylated \(k_w = \sum_{x\in\text{bin } w} q_x\) (note that \(k_w \in \mathbb{R}\) and not \(\mathbb{N}\), since \(q_x \in \mathbb{R}\)), and (iii) the nucleosome occupancy state \(z_w \in \{0,1\}\) that indicates whether a nucleosome has covered the bin (\(z_w = 1\)) or not (\(z_w = 0\)); like \(\sigma_x\), \(z_w\) is hidden and cannot be measured directly.

We model \(k_w\) as drawn from a two-component mixture, protected (\(z_w=1\), call rate \(\theta_P\)) versus accessible (\(z_w=0\), call rate \(\theta_A\)), and fit the mixing weight \(\pi = p(z_w=1)\) together with \(\vec{\theta}=(\theta_P, \theta_A)\) by maximizing the resulting log-likelihood over all bins. Because the occupancy state \(z_w\) is hidden, this maximization cannot be done in closed form; we use the standard expectation-maximization (EM) algorithm to do so iteratively, and we do not reproduce that derivation here. At convergence, the EM algorithm yields the responsibility \(\gamma_w\), i.e., the posterior probability that bin \(w\) is protected given the fitted parameters, from which the parameter estimates follow as weighted averages,

(25)\[\hat{\pi} = \frac{1}{N_{\text{bin}}}\sum_w \gamma_w \;,\qquad \hat{\theta}_P = \frac{\sum_w \gamma_wk_w}{\sum_w \gamma_wM_w} \;,\qquad \hat{\theta}_A = \frac{\sum_w (1-\gamma_w)k_w} {\sum_w (1-\gamma_w)M_w} \;.\]

Because the EM process does not itself distinguish which of the two mixture components is “protected”, it can converge on a set of parameters where \(\hat{\theta}_P > \hat{\theta}_A\); in that case, we swap the values of the two estimators and set \(\hat{\pi} \to 1-\hat{\pi}\). In the package, this fit is controlled by max_iters and tol (the EM iteration cap and convergence tolerance), init_prot/init_acc (its initial guesses for \(\theta_P\)/\(\theta_A\)), and n_min (the minimum number of context-eligible sites a bin needs to be used in the fit).

Handling data with multiple methyltransferase channels

The theory presented thus far focuses on the methylation data from a single type of methyltransferase (MTase). Nevertheless, it is readily generalizable to multiple types of MTases. Indeed, experiments often use more than one kind of MTase to improve signal coverage. The common exogenous DNA MTases used are EcoGII [methyl-6-adenine (m6A)-specific], M.CviPI [GpC-5-methyl-cytosine (5mGpC)-specific], and M.SssI [CpG-5-methyl-cytosine (5mCpG)-specific]. To accommodate these different MTases, we extend the model to have multiple assayable sets \(\{\mathcal{A}_c\}\), where \(c\in\mathcal{M} = \{\text{M6A},\text{HCG},\text{GCH},\text{GCG}\}\) are the methylation channels (H stands for any base except guanine). We need three separate channels for GpC and CpG methylation because GCG are ambiguous sites that can be targeted by both GpC and CpG MTases, and the kinetics there are likely to be different from stand-alone GpC or CpG sites. Every reference coordinate \(x\) belongs to at most one assayable set and these assayable sets do not overlap (i.e., \(\mathcal{A}_c \cap \mathcal{A}_{c'} = \emptyset\) for \(c\neq c'\)), so GCG sites are not double-counted in GCH or HCG channels.

Assuming that the methylation kinetics for these four channels are independent, the log-likelihood ratio \(\Lambda\) can be partitioned by channel. Denoting \(\vec{\theta}_c = (\theta_{P,c},\theta_{A,c})\) as the model parameters for channel \(c \in \mathcal{M}\), the log-likelihood ratio now reads as

(26)\[\Lambda(\mathcal{C},\{\vec{\theta}_c\}) = \sum_i\sum_{c\in\mathcal{M}}\lambda_{i,c}(\vec{\theta}_c) \;,\]

where

(27)\[\begin{split}\lambda_{i,c}(\vec{\theta}_c) = \sum_{\substack{x\in\mathcal{A}_c \\ x \in [x_i,x_i+\ell_{\text{nuc}})}} \log\left(\frac{L_x(q_{x,c},\pi_{0,c})\,\theta_{P,c} + 1-\theta_{P,c}} {L_x(q_{x,c},\pi_{0,c})\,\theta_{A,c} + 1-\theta_{A,c}}\right) \;,\end{split}\]

with \(q_{x,c}\) being the confidence score in calling a site \(x \in \mathcal{A}_c\) methylated in channel \(c\) and \(\pi_{0,c}\) the prior that \(x\) is methylated in that channel (for simplicity, the package sets \(\pi_{0,c} = \pi_0\,\forall c\)). Estimating the parameters \(\vec{\theta}_c\) for each channel can be done in the same way as before, both for the case with control experiments and the case without.

We should also be aware of the following details when considering different MTase channels. First, with in vivo data, both the HCG and GCG channels are contaminated by endogenous methylation at CpG (i.e., 5mCpG), so they should not be used as a readout for accessibility. Second, if an MTase channel is unused in a given experiment, \(\theta_{A,c} \approx \theta_{P,c}\) and \(\lambda_{x,c} \approx 0\), so the theory automatically excludes its contribution to updating the posterior. Finally, we evaluate methylation contexts in a strand-specific manner, and both strands contribute. In practice, the assayable sets \(\{\mathcal{A}_c\}\) and the confidence scores \(q_{x,c}\) are taken from ModKit’s output, using the –ignore-implicit flag so that positions absent from the modification tags are treated as having emitted no call rather than as unmethylated. ModKit searches both strands and reports the strand for each call, so sites on either strand contribute to their respective channels without further processing.

Accounting for correlation in methyltransferase activity between proximal sites

Throughout this section we have made the assumption that the methylation activity at individual assayable sites is independent. This is clearly an oversimplification, as nearby sites can experience crosstalk. For instance, CpG and GpC sites are palindromic, so the methylation signal at the cytosine on one strand will be highly correlated with that on the opposite strand just one base apart. This oversampling inflates the log-likelihood ratio, and we correct for this heuristically by introducing a weighting factor \(\eta\), i.e.,

(28)\[\Lambda(\mathcal{C},\{\vec{\theta}_c\}) \to \eta\,\Lambda(\mathcal{C},\{\vec{\theta}_c\}) \;,\]

and one can think of \(\eta\) as a dial that controls the importance of the data relative to our prior model. By default, the package sets \(\eta = 1\).

While \(\eta=1\) is a reasonable default in the absence of further information, it can also be estimated directly from the data by quantifying how strongly \(\lambda_x\) is autocorrelated along the fiber. If assayable sites were independent, \(\text{Var}(\Lambda)\) would simply be \(\sum_{x\in\mathcal A}\text{Var}(\lambda_x)\); correlation between nearby sites inflates this variance, and \(\eta\) is chosen to undo that inflation. Treating \(\{\lambda_x\}_{x\in\mathcal A}\) as an (approximately) stationary sequence along the fiber with common variance \(\sigma^2=\text{Var}(\lambda_x)\), the variance of the sum is

(29)\[\text{Var}(\Lambda) = \sum_{x,x'\in\mathcal A} \text{Cov}(\lambda_x,\lambda_{x'}) = n\sigma^2\left[1+2\sum_{k=1}^{n-1} \left(1-\frac{k}{n}\right)\rho(k)\right] \;,\]

where \(n=|\mathcal A|\) is the number of assayable, called sites, and

(30)\[\rho(k) = \frac{\displaystyle\sum_{\substack{x,x+k\in\mathcal A}} (\lambda_x-\bar\lambda)(\lambda_{x+k}-\bar\lambda)} {\displaystyle\sum_{x\in\mathcal A}(\lambda_x-\bar\lambda)^2}\]

is the (sample) lag-\(k\) autocorrelation of \(\lambda_x\), with \(\bar\lambda = n^{-1}\sum_{x\in\mathcal A}\lambda_x\) and the numerator summed over pairs of assayable sites exactly \(k\) bp apart. The bracketed factor above is the familiar variance-inflation factor from correlated sampling, and it reduces the effective number of independent sites from \(n\) to \(n_\text{eff}=n/V\), with \(V\) the bracketed term. Choosing

(31)\[\eta = \frac1V = \left[1+2\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\left(1-\frac{k}{n}\right) \rho(k)\right]^{-1}\]

therefore rescales \(\Lambda\) so that its variance matches that of a sum over \(n_\text{eff}\) independent sites, correcting for the oversampling described above. In practice, \(\rho(k)\) is estimated only up to a small cutoff lag (eta_max_lag) beyond which it is indistinguishable from sampling noise, since crosstalk is expected to decay quickly with separation (e.g., the palindromic CpG/GpC correlation above spans just \(k=1\) bp). When multiple MTase channels are in use, the same estimator is applied separately to each channel’s sequence \(\lambda_{x,c}\), giving a channel-specific \(\eta_c\) (by default, estimated from the methylated control when available, or from the test data otherwise; a value can also be pinned per channel via the eta argument).

An empirical alternative to the likelihood

We provide here an alternative approach to estimate the likelihood that does not involve statistical modeling, which may not be suitable for conditions where single-molecule footprinting data are not available. One such scenario is when there are only bulk accessibility data, such as those based on MNase-seq.

For a molecule \(\alpha\), we start from the raw footprinting signal \(S_{x,\alpha}\) (e.g., the confidence score \(q_{x,\alpha}\) used above) and smooth it with a left-aligned rolling average of bin size \(\ell_\text{bin}\):

(32)\[\widetilde S_{x,\alpha} = \frac{1}{\ell_\text{bin}} \sum_{i=0}^{\ell_\text{bin}-1} S_{x+i,\alpha} \;,\]

so that \(\widetilde S_{x,\alpha}\) depends only on sites \(x,x+1,\dots,x+\ell_\text{bin}-1\); we typically take \(\ell_\text{bin}=\ell_{\text{nuc}}=147\) bp [1].

Averaging \(\widetilde S_{x,\alpha}\) over all molecules of a given experimental condition \(Y\in\{\text{test},\text{meth}, \text{unmeth}\}\) (cf. the saturated-MTase and no-MTase control experiments above) gives \(\widetilde S_x^{(Y)} = \big(N_\text{mol}^{(Y)}\big)^{-1}\sum_\alpha \widetilde S_{x,\alpha}^{(Y)}\). When both controls are available, we normalize the (molecule-level) test signal relative to them,

(33)\[M_{x,\alpha} = \frac{\widetilde S_{x,\alpha}^{(\text{test})} - \widetilde S_x^{(\text{unmeth})}}{\widetilde S_x^{(\text{meth})} - \widetilde S_x^{(\text{unmeth})}} \;,\]

so that \(M_{x,\alpha}\) tends towards 0 where the test molecule resembles the unmethylated (protected) control and towards 1 where it resembles the fully methylated (accessible) control. If no control experiments are available, we instead set \(M_{x,\alpha} = \widetilde S_{x,\alpha}^{(\text{test})}\).

To convert \(M_{x,\alpha}\) into a formal probability \(p^{(\text{meth})}_{x,\alpha}\in[0,1]\) that site \(x\) is methylated (i.e., accessible) on molecule \(\alpha\), we linearly rescale \(M_{x,\alpha}\) against two percentile bounds, clipping values that fall outside of them:

(34)\[p^{(\text{meth})}_{x,\alpha} = \text{clip}\!\left[ \frac{M_{x,\alpha} - M^{(\text{unmeth})}_{p=0.1\%}} {M^{(\text{meth})}_{p=99.9\%} - M^{(\text{unmeth})}_{p=0.1\%}}, \,0,\,1\right] \;,\]

where \(M^{(\text{unmeth})}_{p=0.1\%}\) is the 0.1th percentile and \(M^{(\text{meth})}_{p=99.9\%}\) the 99.9th percentile (the default choice used in the package) of the normalized score \(M_{x,\alpha}\), each estimated once per fiber, but deliberately from different sources: the lower bound \(M^{(\text{unmeth})}_{p=0.1\%}\) is taken over a random subsample of molecules from the unmeth control channel alone, and the upper bound \(M^{(\text{meth})}_{p=99.9\%}\) over a random subsample from the meth control channel alone, so that each bound is anchored to the condition it is meant to represent rather than to the (possibly differently distributed) test signal. Both percentiles are pooled over all sites \(x\) within their respective sample rather than estimated separately at each site. When no controls are available, both bounds are instead estimated from the test channel itself, i.e., \(M^{(\text{unmeth})}_{p=0.1\%}\to M^{(\text{test})}_{p=0.1\%}\) and \(M^{(\text{meth})}_{p=99.9\%}\to M^{(\text{test})}_{p=99.9\%}\).

As above, we identify the complementary probability \(p^{(\text{seq})}_{x,\alpha} = 1-p^{(\text{meth})}_{x,\alpha}\) as the (empirical) probability that \(x\) is occupied by a nucleosome on molecule \(\alpha\). To turn \(p^{(\text{seq})}\) into an energy, we treat the site as a two-state system – occupied, with probability \(p^{(\text{seq})}\), versus accessible, with probability \(1-p^{(\text{seq})}=p^{(\text{meth})}\) – and take their relative Boltzmann weight to define an energy difference \(E^{(\text{seq})}\) (in units of \(k_BT\), i.e., setting \(\beta=1\)):

(35)\[\frac{p^{(\text{seq})}_{x,\alpha}}{1-p^{(\text{seq})}_{x,\alpha}} = \exp[-E^{(\text{seq})}_{x,\alpha}] \;,\]

having set the accessible state as the reference (zero-energy) state. Inverting this, and clipping the result to a maximum magnitude \(E_\text{max}\) to prevent it from diverging as \(p^{(\text{meth})}\to\{0,1\}\), we arrive at

(36)\[\begin{split}E^{(\text{seq})}_{x,\alpha} &= \text{clip}\!\left[ -\log \frac{p^{(\text{seq})}_{x,\alpha}}{1-p^{(\text{seq})}_{x,\alpha}}, \,-E_\text{max},\,E_\text{max}\right] \\ &= \text{clip}\!\left[\log \frac{p^{(\text{meth})}_{x,\alpha}} {1-p^{(\text{meth})}_{x,\alpha}}, \,-E_\text{max},\,E_\text{max}\right] \;,\end{split}\]

i.e., \(E^{(\text{seq})}\) is simply the (clipped) log-odds, or logit, of \(p^{(\text{meth})}\). This differs from a naive Boltzmann inversion \(-k_BT\log p^{(\text{seq})}_x\) in that it is referenced against the complementary (accessible) state rather than against a hypothetical zero-energy vacuum, which keeps \(E^{(\text{seq})}\) finite and antisymmetric about \(p^{(\text{meth})}=p^{(\text{seq})}=1/2\) (where it is favourable neither to bind nor not to bind); the two forms would coincide only in the limit \(p^{(\text{meth})}\ll1\). Like the statistical model above, this construction is applied independently to each molecule \(\alpha\), so every molecule is assigned its own empirical energy landscape \(E^{(\text{seq})}_{x,\alpha}\) prior to running its Monte Carlo simulation.

The full Hamiltonian

We can now combine the prior above with either likelihood model discussed above (the statistical, emission-based likelihood, or the empirical alternative) into a single effective Hamiltonian for use in the Monte Carlo sampling. Recalling that the posterior takes the Boltzmann form \(P(\mathcal C|\text{data})\propto \exp[-\beta H(\mathcal C)]\), and that the repulsion and chemical-potential terms of the prior enter \(H\) unchanged, we write

(37)\[H(\mathcal C) = E_\text{data}(\mathcal C) + E_\text{rep}(\mathcal C) - \mu N \;,\]

where \(E_\text{data}(\mathcal C)\) depends on which likelihood model is used:

(38)\[\begin{split}E_\text{data}(\mathcal C) = \begin{cases} \displaystyle -k_BT\,\Lambda(\mathcal C,\vec\theta) = -k_BT\sum_{i=1}^N \lambda_i(\vec\theta) & \text{(statistical model)} \;,\\[2ex] \displaystyle \sum_{i=1}^N E^{(\text{seq})}_{x_i,\alpha} & \text{(empirical alternative)} \;. \end{cases}\end{split}\]

In both cases, \(E_\text{data}\) is a sum of one-body contributions, one per nucleosome, that depend only on that nucleosome’s own position \(x_i\) and can therefore be precomputed for a given molecule before the simulation starts: in the statistical model, the contribution \(\lambda_i(\vec\theta)\) sums the site-level log-odds over the nucleosome’s entire footprint \([x_i,x_i+\ell_{\text{nuc}})\), whereas in the empirical alternative, the contribution \(E^{(\text{seq})}_{x_i,\alpha}\) is read off directly from the smoothed and normalized signal at the nucleosome’s left edge \(x_i\) alone.

In fact, both routes only ever enter the Hamiltonian by way of a methylation probability \(p^{(\text{meth})}_{x,\alpha}\), which is then turned into an energy by the identical clipped log-odds transform above – what differs between the two likelihood models is only how \(p^{(\text{meth})}_{x,\alpha}\) itself is obtained. In the empirical alternative, \(p^{(\text{meth})}_{x,\alpha}\) is the smoothed, control-normalized, percentile-clipped score above. In the statistical model, we instead read it off the emission model: generalizing the one-body term \(\lambda_i(\vec\theta)\) – there defined only at an existing nucleosome’s index \(i\), summing the site-level log-odds over its footprint \([x_i,x_i+\ell_{\text{nuc}})\) – to an arbitrary candidate position \(x\),

(39)\[\begin{split}\lambda(x;\vec\theta) = \sum_{\substack{x'\in\mathcal{A} \\ x' \in [x,x+\ell_{\text{nuc}})}} \log\left(\frac{L_{x'}(q_{x'},\pi_0)\,\theta_P + 1-\theta_P} {L_{x'}(q_{x'},\pi_0)\,\theta_A + 1-\theta_A}\right) \;,\end{split}\]

so that \(\lambda_i(\vec\theta) \equiv \lambda(x_i;\vec\theta)\), we take

(40)\[p^{(\text{meth})}_{x,\alpha} = \frac{1}{1+\exp[\lambda(x;\vec\theta)]} \;,\]

i.e., the emission model’s log-odds ratio \(\lambda(x;\vec\theta)\) is itself treated as a log-odds and inverted into a probability, exactly as \(M_{x,\alpha}\) is rescaled into a probability in the empirical route. Substituting this into the clipped log-odds transform above and using \(\log[p/(1-p)] = -\lambda(x;\vec\theta)\) for \(p=p^{(\text{meth})}_{x,\alpha}\), we find

(41)\[E^{(\text{seq})}_{x,\alpha} = \text{clip}\!\left[-\lambda(x;\vec\theta), \,-E_\text{max},\,E_\text{max}\right] \qquad \text{(statistical model)} \;.\]

Comparing this with the Hamiltonian \(\beta H = -\Lambda(\mathcal C,\vec\theta)+\beta[E_\text{rep}(\mathcal C)-\mu N]+ \text{const.}\) above, we see that using the emission-model likelihood is, up to the additive constant already noted there, the same as taking \(E_\text{data}(\mathcal C) = -k_BT\,\Lambda(\mathcal C,\vec\theta) = -k_BT\sum_{i=1}^N\lambda_i(\vec\theta)\) – except that each one-body term is now clipped to \(\pm E_\text{max}\) before being summed, precisely as in the empirical alternative. This detail was left implicit above: without it, an occasional site with an extreme log-odds ratio \(\lambda(x;\vec\theta)\) (e.g., from a run of highly confident calls) could otherwise dominate the Hamiltonian and destabilize the sampling, so both likelihood routes cap their one-body contribution the same way before it enters \(E_\text{data}(\mathcal C)\).

In both cases, \(E_\text{data}\) is therefore a sum of identically-structured one-body contributions, one per nucleosome, that depend only on that nucleosome’s own position \(x_i\) (and the choice of likelihood model used to obtain \(p^{(\text{meth})}_{x, \alpha}\) there) and can be precomputed for a given molecule before the simulation starts. This is the Hamiltonian we sample in the Monte Carlo simulations described next.

Sampling dynamics – Markov Chain Monte Carlo

Having assembled the full Hamiltonian \(H(\mathcal C)\) above, we now turn to how nucleosome configurations are sampled from the posterior. Since we allow the number of nucleosomes \(N\) to vary, the natural statistical ensemble is the grand canonical ensemble, in which, at thermal equilibrium, a configuration \(\mathcal C\) is observed with probability

(42)\[P(\mathcal C) = \frac1Z \exp[-\beta H(\mathcal C)] \;,\]

\(Z\) being the partition function and \(\beta=1/(k_BT)\). We sample this distribution with a Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) procedure, performing one of the following three moves at each step.

  1. Shifting a nucleosome. We randomly select the \(i\)-th nucleosome and propose to move it by one bp, \(x_i\to x_i'=x_i\pm1\). The move is accepted with probability

    (43)\[p(x_i\to x_i') = \min\Big\{1, \exp[-\beta(\Delta E_\text{data}+ \Delta E_\text{rep})]\Big\} \;,\]

    where \(\Delta E_\text{data}\) and \(\Delta E_\text{rep}\) are the changes in the data and repulsion energies caused by the move (the number of nucleosomes, and hence the \(-\mu N\) term, is unchanged).

  2. Adding a nucleosome. We randomly select a candidate position \(x\) from the \(n_\text{pos}=L-\ell_{\text{nuc}}\) possible (left-aligned) positions on the fiber [2]. The move is accepted with probability

    (44)\[p(\text{add at }x) = \min\left\{1,\frac{n_\text{pos}}{N+1} \exp[\beta\mu-\beta\Delta E_\text{add}(x)]\right\} \;,\]

    where \(\Delta E_\text{add}(x) = E_\text{data}(x)+ \Delta E_\text{rep}\) is the change in the data and repulsion energies from placing a nucleosome at \(x\), and the prefactor \(n_\text{pos}/(N+1)\) corrects for the asymmetry between proposing a new position out of \(n_\text{pos}\) choices and proposing its removal out of the resulting \(N+1\) nucleosomes.

  3. Removing a nucleosome. We randomly select the \(i\)-th nucleosome (out of \(N\)) for removal. The move is accepted with probability

    (45)\[p(\text{remove }i) = \min\left\{1,\frac{N}{n_\text{pos}} \exp[-\beta\mu-\beta\Delta E_\text{remove}(x_i)]\right\} \;,\]

    where \(\Delta E_\text{remove}(x_i) = -E_\text{data}(x_i) + \Delta E_\text{rep}\) is the change in the data and repulsion energies from removing the nucleosome at \(x_i\).

These moves satisfy detailed balance with respect to \(P(\mathcal C)\) and together define a Metropolis algorithm that drives the ensemble of nucleosome configurations towards thermal equilibrium. In practice, we run the chain under a temperature annealing schedule, with \(\beta^{-1}\) decreasing from a starting value to a final target value – linearly, geometrically, or held constant – over the course of the simulation, which helps the chain avoid metastable configurations before it settles into equilibrium at the target temperature.
